# AP style notes for weekly quizzes

## Numbers

Spell out the numbers one through nine, and use figures for 10 and above. Always use figures for ages, percentages, dollar amounts, dimensions, and scores, even when the number is below 10: a 5-year-old boy, 3 percent, $4. Spell out a number when it starts a sentence, except for years. Write "percent" as a word in running text, not the % symbol.

## Dates and times

Abbreviate the months Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov. and Dec. when they are used with a specific date, as in Oct. 4. Spell out March, April, May, June and July in every case. Do not use "st," "nd," "rd," or "th" after dates. Use a.m. and p.m. in lowercase with periods, and use noon and midnight instead of 12 p.m. and 12 a.m. Do not write 7:00 p.m.; write 7 p.m.

## Titles

Capitalize a formal title when it comes directly before a name, as in President Maria Lopez. Lowercase the title when it comes after the name or stands alone: Maria Lopez, the president of the student council. Lowercase job descriptions that are not formal titles, such as coach or reporter, even before a name.

## Addresses

Use the abbreviations Ave., Blvd. and St. only with a numbered address, as in 1845 Sheridan Road or 600 Davis St. Spell them out when there is no number: Davis Street. Road, Drive and Lane are never abbreviated.

## States and cities

In the body of a story, spell out the names of all states. Some large cities, such as Chicago, Boston and Los Angeles, stand alone without a state name in datelines and stories. Smaller cities need the state: Evanston, Illinois.

## Punctuation

AP style does not use the Oxford comma in a simple series: red, white and blue. Use it only when a series contains complex phrases that would otherwise be confusing. Periods and commas always go inside quotation marks. Use one space after a period, never two.

## Commonly confused words

"Affect" is usually a verb, and "effect" is usually a noun. "Fewer" is for things you can count, and "less" is for amounts you cannot count. "Who" refers to people, and "that" refers to things. Use "more than" with numbers, as in more than 200 students, and "over" for spatial relationships.

## Composition titles

Put quotation marks around the titles of books, movies, songs, TV shows and video games. Do not use quotation marks or italics for the names of newspapers and magazines, or for reference books like the AP Stylebook itself.
