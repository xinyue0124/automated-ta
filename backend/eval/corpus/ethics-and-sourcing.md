# Lecture notes: Ethics, sourcing and verification

## Finding sources

Good stories need people who have direct knowledge: witnesses, people affected by a decision, and the officials who made it. Start with documents, such as meeting minutes, budgets, court records and police reports, and then find the people named in them. Public records are often more reliable than memory.

Seek out sources who disagree with each other. A story about a new parking fee should include the city official who proposed it and the residents or business owners who will pay it. Diversity of sources matters too: if every source in your story looks and sounds the same, you have missed part of the community.

## Interviewing

Prepare for every interview by researching the person and writing your questions in advance. Ask open questions that begin with how, why, or what happened, rather than questions that can be answered with yes or no. Listen more than you talk, and ask follow-up questions when an answer is vague.

At the end of every interview, ask two questions: "Is there anything I didn't ask that I should have?" and "Who else should I talk to?" Always confirm the spelling of the person's name and their exact title before you hang up.

Tell sources you are a student reporter working on a story for class and that it may be published. Do not interview friends, family members, or roommates for class stories.

## On the record, off the record and on background

By default, everything a source tells a reporter is on the record and can be published with their name. "Off the record" means the information cannot be published at all; it may only guide your reporting. "On background" means you can use the information but cannot name the source, and you describe them in a general way, such as "a city employee."

An agreement to go off the record must be made before the source says the information, not after. If a source says something and then asks for it to be off the record, you can decide whether to agree, but the default is that it was already on the record. For this class, check with the instructor before you agree to any off-the-record or background arrangement.

## Anonymous sources

Use anonymous sources only when the information is important, cannot be obtained any other way, and the source faces real harm, such as losing a job or physical danger, if named. Explain in the story why the source was not named. In this class, you must tell the instructor the identity of any anonymous source before your story is graded.

## Verification

Verify every fact with a second source or a document. For claims from social media, find the original post, check who posted it and when, and contact the person directly. A photo or video can be old or from another place: use a reverse image search to check where it first appeared.

If two sources disagree about a fact, report the disagreement and keep reporting until you can find a document that settles it. Do not split the difference.

## Conflicts of interest

Do not write about organizations you belong to, such as a club, team, or employer. If you are unsure, disclose the connection to the instructor before you start reporting. Do not accept gifts, free tickets, or meals from people you cover; a cup of coffee during an interview is the only exception.

## Minimizing harm

Ethical reporting weighs the public's need to know against possible harm to the people in the story. Be especially careful with minors, victims of crimes, and people in grief. Do not name a minor accused of a crime. When you report on suicide, follow safe reporting guidelines: do not describe the method, and include information about where readers can find help.

## Corrections

When you make a mistake, admit it quickly and fix it in the open. A correction states what was wrong and what is right, without repeating the error in a way that spreads it further.
