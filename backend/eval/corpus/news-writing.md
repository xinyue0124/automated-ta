# Lecture notes: Writing the news story

## The inverted pyramid

Most news stories are written in the inverted pyramid: the most important information comes first, and each paragraph after that is less important than the one before it. Editors used to cut stories from the bottom to fit the page, so the ending had to be expendable. Today the reason is readers: most people read only the first few paragraphs, so those paragraphs must carry the news.

The inverted pyramid answers the five W's and H — who, what, when, where, why, and how — as early as possible. You do not need all six in the first sentence. Pick the two or three that matter most for this story.

## Writing the lede

The lede is the first sentence or paragraph of the story. A hard news lede tells the reader what happened in one sentence of about 25 to 35 words. Lead with the newest and most consequential fact, not with background. Avoid starting a lede with a date, a time, or the name of an unknown person, because those are rarely the news.

A delayed lede, also called an anecdotal lede, opens with a scene, a person, or a small story, and reveals the news a few paragraphs later. Use delayed ledes for features and longer stories, not for breaking news. If you cannot explain in one sentence why your anecdote matters, use a hard news lede instead.

## The nut graf

In a story with a delayed lede, the nut graf is the paragraph that explains why the story matters right now. It usually appears in the third to fifth paragraph. A good nut graf connects the opening scene to the larger news and tells the reader what the rest of the story will cover. Without a nut graf, readers lose patience and stop reading.

## Quotes and attribution

Use direct quotes for strong opinions, emotion, or memorable language. Paraphrase facts and routine statements; a quote that says "the meeting starts at 7 p.m." wastes the reader's time. Never change the words inside quotation marks, even to fix grammar. If a quote needs fixing, paraphrase it instead.

Attribute every piece of information that you did not witness yourself. In news writing, "said" is almost always the right verb. Words like "claimed," "admitted," or "insisted" carry judgments, so avoid them unless they are literally accurate. Put the attribution after the first sentence of a long quote, not at the end of the whole quote.

On first reference, use a source's full name and title. On second reference, use only the last name, for everyone, regardless of age or position.

## Numbers in the story

Readers struggle with long strings of numbers. Use no more than one or two numbers in a sentence, and round when precision does not matter. Always put a number in context: a 10 percent budget cut means more if you say what it pays for. Check every number twice, and do the math yourself instead of trusting a source's percentage.

## Story length and paragraphs

Newspaper paragraphs are short, usually one to three sentences. Short paragraphs are easier to read on a phone. A news brief runs 150 to 250 words, a standard news story runs 500 to 700 words, and a feature for this class runs about 900 words.

## Writing the ending

News stories in the inverted pyramid do not need a conclusion. End with the least important information, or with a quote that looks ahead. Features are different: they often end with a kicker, a final quote or detail that echoes the lede and gives the reader a sense of closure. Never end a story with your own opinion.
